# Spinalonga: why it was fortified and how the island’s uses changed – updated research report

## Executive assessment

Spinalonga (officially part of the Kalydon island–peninsula unit) is a natural rocky islet at the northern entrance of the Gulf of Elounda, opposite Plaka and below the Kalydon/Spinalonga peninsula; it was not created by cutting a channel in Venetian times. Its position controls the passage between the enclosed waters of Elounda and the wider approaches of Mirabello Bay, making it a natural lookout and seaward bastion for any power wishing to supervise movement, taxation and conflict in this stretch of the Cretan north‑east coast.[^1][^2][^3][^4]

Across the long span covered in this report, the same terrain has been adapted in at least five major ways: as a late antique/Byzantine defensive outpost guarding the gulf, as a late sixteenth‑century Venetian fortress, as a Venetian enclave after most of Crete had fallen to the Ottomans, as an Ottoman Muslim settlement and commercial port, as a compulsory isolation institution for people with Hansen’s disease (leprosy), and finally as an archaeological/heritage and tourism site. Each phase left physical and archival traces that overlap and sometimes erase one another; the core task is to read those layers together rather than telling a single, flattened story of “the leper island”.[^5][^4][^6]

The existing Codex dossier is broadly sound on the main dates, institutional decisions and physical phases, especially where it follows Ephorate of Antiquities of Lasithi (EAL) material and UNESCO’s Tentative List documentation, but key legal texts, Ottoman demographic data, local testimony and some infrastructure chronologies were missing. This update confirms several baseline statements, corrects or nuances others (notably the 1903–04 legal sequence, Ottoman population figures and UNESCO status), and identifies further primary sources that should be consulted before drafting the public article.[^7][^8][^4][^5]

At this stage the project remains **conditionally ready** for drafting: the overall structure, periodisation and avoidance of sensationalism are now well grounded, but the publication gate should stay closed until the core Cretan State legislation has been seen in facsimile, at least one Ottoman‑period population/property study is incorporated, and additional named testimonies from residents, workers and mainland communities have been secured.

### Additional Codex points cross‑checked

The Codex dossier also advanced working positions on pre‑Venetian occupation, Ottoman settlement scale, medical chronology and etymology. These are broadly compatible with current institutional sources but require slight recalibration:

- The re‑dating of the “ancient” wall to the 7th–8th centuries CE and association with defence against Arab raiding is affirmed in the EAL’s Byzantine‑times material; the dossier was right to reject an unqualified “classical fortress” narrative and to flag the need for an excavation report.[^24][^4]
- Ottoman population figures around 1112 residents in 1881, about 200 houses and 25 shops stem from UNESCO Nomination texts and later syntheses; EAL’s thematic page on daily life in the Ottoman settlement now offers a more cautious range (800–1400 early in the century, 1112 inhabitants and 227 families in the late 1870s–80s, with municipal status in 1886) based on Ottoman‑period demographic research.[^4][^25]
- The medical‑historical outline in the Codex, which distinguishes early deprivation, later collective organisation by residents (including the Brotherhood of the Sick of Spinalonga) and the introduction of sulfone treatment in the 1940s, is supported by Stavrakakis’s peer‑reviewed article and the International Leprosy Association archive.[^8][^18]
- The cautious etymology classification (Spinalonga from “stin Elounda” as plausible but unproven; Italian *spina lunga* and “island’s shape” as folk or secondary) remains appropriate in light of later popular and semi‑scholarly treatments, which tend to recycle both explanations without returning to Venetian‑era documents.[^26][^27]

## Strategic–geographical explanation

Spinalonga stands at the narrow northern entrance to the Gulf of Elounda, immediately offshore from Plaka and in line with routes that run along the Mirabello coast towards Agios Nikolaos and further east. Any artillery placed on the island’s summit or on its bastioned seaward walls commands both the sheltered water inside the gulf and the slightly wider but still constrained waters between the island and the Kalydon peninsula, offering layered control over shipping approaching from the north‑east, north‑west or along the gulf itself.[^21][^2][^37][^27]

In Venetian strategic thinking of the late sixteenth century, this position complemented other fortified points such as Souda and Gramvousa, forming part of a chain of seaward bastions that guarded harbours, anchorages and economically important installations such as salt pans. The EAL material notes nearby salt‑production around Elounda but stops short of asserting that salt alone explains the fort’s construction, instead placing emphasis on a wider “defensive programme” against Ottoman expansion; the report therefore retains a combined explanation of harbour control, economic stakes and Ottoman threat, and avoids a single‑cause narrative.[^38][^2][^3][^5]

The islet’s relationship with Elounda and Plaka is not merely visual. The short crossing from Plaka (hundreds of metres) makes it a practical embarkation point for supplies and workers in all periods once small craft and, later, motor boats are available. In the leprosarium era, however, the social meanings of this proximity were shaped by legal isolation and stigma: food, goods and people crossed the water under strict regulation, and the gulf that now sees tourist boats marked a boundary between confinement and mainland communities.[^39][^18][^37][^33]

## Fortress construction and architectural phases

### Venetian design logic and phases

The Venetian fortress on Spinalonga is a classic example of late sixteenth‑century bastioned seaward fortification adapted to a rocky island setting. The design responded to three main conditions:[^9][^3]

- The need to prevent hostile landings on the island’s shorelines.
- The need to mount artillery that could command approaches in several directions.
- The limited resources available for construction on a relatively small, uneven site.

The EAL construction‑phases account, echoed by a Ministry booklet, describes a first phase (1579–86) in which, following Bressani’s design, a perimeter coastal wall was constructed with demi‑lune bastions at critical points and small fortifications at the island’s high spots to deny enemy landings. This produced a strong but low‑lying belt of masonry whose lines were still vulnerable to fire from higher ground on the peninsula.[^2][^9]

In 1584, Latino Orsini inspected the works and judged that the fortress could be attacked from the south, prompting a second phase (1585–86) that fortified the rocky ridge with a wall and connected this high line to the lower enclosure through transverse walls on the north‑east and south‑west, thereby creating a two‑tier defensive system that reduced the length of coastal wall still to be built. Later repairs and modifications during the Cretan War adjusted bastions and artillery positions but did not fundamentally change this dual‑zone pattern.[^10][^37][^9]

### Surviving features and reading the fabric

Today, visitors can trace the lower sea wall, bastions, demi‑lunes and casemated gun positions that curve around much of the island, as well as the ridge‑top wall and connecting transverse works that frame the interior settlement terraces. Cisterns, magazines, barracks and gate structures survive in various states of repair, some re‑used in Ottoman and leprosarium periods, making it important to distinguish fabric by phase rather than treating all masonry as a single “castle”.[^3][^5][^21][^2]

EAL documentation emphasises that the fortress functioned as an integrated **system**, not simply a collection of picturesque ruins: the arrangement of batteries, the relationship between inner and outer lines, and the siting of cisterns and magazines all express Venetian priorities in sustaining a garrison under siege and controlling fire zones around the gulf. Any public explanation should therefore walk readers through the logic of the plan as much as through individual named bastions.[^2]

## Venetian enclave and community after 1669

When most of Crete passed to Ottoman control in 1669, Spinalonga, Souda and Gramvousa remained in Venetian hands under the terms of the Candia settlement, forming a small chain of outposts that continued to fly the Lion of St Mark for several decades. These enclaves served as bases for Venetian naval operations, refuges for Cretan Christians and points of friction with the new Ottoman administration on the mainland.[^27][^3]

While EAL and derivative accounts clearly state that Spinalonga remained under Venetian rule until 1715, they offer limited detail on civilian populations, provisioning systems and interactions with nearby communities during this period; many later narratives of heroism and suffering rely on much later romantic or nationalist sources. Before any website article asserts specific episodes of resistance, famine or population numbers for this enclave phase, further archival research in Venetian Provveditori reports and Ottoman correspondence is required; until then, the safest path is to state the enclave status, explain that it was supplied by sea and contested, and note that social history inside the walls remains underdocumented.[^11][^25]

## Ottoman settlement history

### Scale, composition and economy

After the 1715 surrender, Ottoman authorities repaired the fortress and gradually settled Janissaries and their families on the island, transforming it into a Muslim settlement with both military and civilian functions. EAL’s thematic piece on daily life in the Ottoman settlement estimates the early 19th‑century population between 800 and 1400 individuals and records a population of 1112 inhabitants organised into 227 families by the late 1870s–80s; it also notes that Spinalonga was recognised as a municipality in 1886.[^40][^11][^4]

These figures dovetail with Cretan‑history syntheses and local studies that speak of around 1110–1112 people, almost all Muslims, in the 1881 census and identify Spinalonga as one of the largest Muslim towns in Mirabello. Several tourism‑oriented sites add claims of “200 houses and 25 shops”, citing UNESCO nomination material; EAL confirms that shops and workshops lined the harbour front and that trade in salt, leather, honey, wax, grindstones, silk and carobs is documented, but the exact counts of houses and shops should still be attributed cautiously to nomination texts until the underlying census and property records are examined.[^41][^42][^25][^4]

### Physical form and everyday life

Architecturally, the Ottoman settlement adapted and extended existing Venetian structures, inserting stepped houses along the west and south slopes, building single‑ and two‑storey dwellings in rows along narrow streets, and converting parts of the lower enclosure into a dense residential and commercial quarter. EAL notes the presence of mosques, a minaret (now lost), a hammam, fountains and an Ottoman cemetery on the island’s eastern side, together with burial grounds on the opposite shore and the peninsula; some tombstones were later re‑used as building material in the leprosarium period, traces of which can be seen in the exhibition area.[^28][^6][^4]

Municipal records and historical essays outline a lively harbour economy: Ottoman Spinalonga exported local produce, acted as a customs point, and, according to later testimonies, also supported smuggling and small‑scale piracy, though these latter activities are harder to corroborate from official documents. Daily life included religious practice in mosques, schooling, trade, and family routines comparable to other Cretan Muslim communities of the time; these dimensions deserve more attention in Ottomanist and local‑history research so that the island is not remembered only for its final medical phase.[^43][^44]

### Departure of the Muslim population

The transition from Ottoman settlement to abandoned housing stock in the early 20th century followed the wider unravelling of Ottoman Crete and the creation of the autonomous Cretan State. A detailed Greek article on Venetian and Ottoman Spinalonga notes that by the 1881 census virtually all of the island’s 1111 inhabitants were Muslims (1110 Muslims and one Christian woman), but that after the departure of the Ottoman army and the establishment of the Cretan State in 1898, most Muslim residents left the island; by the first Cretan State census the population had dropped to 272, with a small Christian minority.[^25]

The EAL modern‑times text explicitly states that the 1903 decision to establish the leprosarium was motivated both by the presence of abandoned Ottoman houses and by the political intention to remove the remaining Muslim population. This dual motive—medical/public‑health and demographic/political—should be discussed clearly in the future article, differentiating documented state policy from later teleological narratives that present the leprosarium solely as an act of humanitarian care.[^1]

## Establishment of the leprosy settlement

### Legal acts and institutional framework

The legal framework emerged in stages:

- A 1901 Cretan law (Law 375) “On the isolation of lepers in Crete” established compulsory notification, authorised the appointment of a medical inspector, and set general measures for segregation; a modern thesis reproduces this law from a handwritten copy in an appendix, confirming its seven‑article structure.[^29][^30]
- Law 463 “On the Settlement of the Lepers of Crete”, voted on 30 May 1903 and published in OGGCS vol. C’, issue 25, 7 June 1903, specifically designated Spinalonga as the place where all people with leprosy of Crete were to be settled and transferred funds for the establishment and maintenance of the institution.[^13][^29]
- Regulatory Decree 166, voted on 18 November 1903 and published in OGGCS vol. D’, issue 25, set out the organisational framework of the leprosarium, including administrative structures, staff roles and procedures.[^13]
- A further decree, “On the internal operation of the leper colony”, printed in OGGCS Year E’, 1903, p. 419, supplemented the earlier provisions with detailed internal regulations.[^14]

These acts collectively created a compulsory isolation regime in which all persons identified with leprosy in Crete were to be transferred to Spinalonga, under the authority of the Cretan State and later, after 1913, the Greek State. Until high‑quality scans or official transcriptions from the Gazette are integrated into the project, the article must summarise this framework using cautious paraphrase and clear attribution rather than quoting legal clauses verbatim.[^18][^1]

### Reasons for selecting Spinalonga

EAL’s modern‑times article frames the choice of Spinalonga in two main ways: as aligning with “the modern framework of the time” that dictated isolation of people with leprosy away from the “healthy” population, and as exploiting an island with existing, now‑abandoned Ottoman houses on which the remaining Muslim residents could be expelled. Greek historical essays and Ministry cultural material echo this dual rationale, emphasising both public‑health discourse (fear of contagion, stigma, and the perceived need for quarantine) and demographic/political motives (consolidating Christian control over a former Muslim stronghold).[^45][^46][^1]

Compared with alternative sites considered in Cretan debates, such as Dionysades, Spinalonga offered a combination of relative proximity to administrative centres (via Heraklion and Mirabello), existing built fabric that reduced initial costs, and the symbolic effect of re‑purposing a former Ottoman town into a state medical institution. These factors should be described in analytical rather than moralising terms, making clear that the decision reflected both contemporary medical thinking and power relations on Crete at the time.[^46]

## Human‑scale account of life inside

### Early years: deprivation and stigma

Stavrakakis’s article in Archives of Hellenic Medicine and the EAL leprosarium‑foundation page depict the first decade of the colony as marked by severe deprivation. Residents lived in dilapidated former Ottoman houses, many without adequate sanitation or protection from weather; they depended on inconsistent state rations and charitable donations, often supplemented by begging or informal work. Medical care focused on isolation rather than cure, as effective sulfone treatment was not yet available; many patients experienced social death, having their names struck from municipal registers and being regarded as “dead” by their home communities, a practice attested in both medical history and later testimonies.[^47][^15][^8][^18][^29]

Stigma and fear discouraged visits from relatives and facilitated a regime in which residents had limited voice; several sources describe the early community as fragmented and oriented primarily towards individual survival rather than collective organisation. This picture must be presented without sensational language, using period terminology only in quotation or contextual explanation.[^48][^18]

### Self‑organisation, work and social life

From the mid‑1930s, the arrival of Epaminondas Remoundakis and the formation of the Brotherhood changed the social dynamics. EAL’s biographical article on Remoundakis sets out a clear, institutionally supported narrative: born in Sitia in 1914, he studied law in Athens, was confined to Agia Varvara’s leprosy hospital, then voluntarily transferred to Spinalonga in March 1936, where his presence helped catalyse the creation of the “Brotherhood of the Sick of Spinalonga, Saint Panteleimon”, the first association of its kind in Greece.[^34][^49]

The Brotherhood’s charter, as summarised by EAL, aimed to improve living conditions and to secure “all means of culture that could alleviate the misery and despair of the sick”, and its activism led to concrete gains: the opening of the perimeter road in 1939 (recorded in an inscription crediting the Brotherhood and Remoundakis), improvements in housing, organised cultural events, and greater leverage in negotiations with authorities. Secondary accounts, sometimes based on Remoundakis’s posthumously published autobiography *An Eagle Without Wings*, expand this picture with references to painted houses, planted trees, small shops, cafés, a barber, a theatre, a cinema and even loudspeakers playing music, but these details should be used only when tied back to identifiable testimonies.[^35][^50]

Work on Spinalonga spanned domestic labour, small‑scale agriculture in available plots, maintenance of infrastructure, crafts and services (such as barbering or small food outlets), and limited remunerated employment funded by the institution’s budget; exact employment rosters, however, have not yet been integrated into this project and remain a gap. Education for children and literacy programmes for adults are mentioned in oral histories and some travel narratives, but require verification from school or church records before forming firm part of the article’s narrative.[^36][^18]

### Families, marriages and communication

Available scholarly and institutional sources confirm that families were formed and marriages took place on Spinalonga, sometimes between residents and sometimes involving partners from outside who chose to join the community, though patterns vary over time. Children were born on the island, some of whom were removed for fostering or institutional care; exact numbers, policies and outcomes are sensitive topics and need further documentation from state and church records.[^36][^18]

Communication with relatives on the mainland took place through letters, occasional visits (subject to health regulations and stigma), and mediated contacts via staff or clergy. Several testimonies in Greek media and oral‑history projects describe the emotional toll of separation and the ways in which residents sought to maintain ties, but this material has not yet been systematically collected for the present project; for now, only the general fact of constrained but real communication should be stated.[^18][^34]

### Burials, cemetery and ossuary

EAL’s page on the leper colony cemetery explains that from the leprosarium’s early years, the square of the Donato bastion was used as a burial ground; as space became insufficient, cannon ports were removed and concrete graves constructed, and a small ossuary was added on the bastion’s northern side for bones exhumed from earlier graves. Later journalistic and ethnographic pieces describe tourists in the 1980s disturbing remains in a communal grave and the eventual 2013 rebuilding of the ossuary under church auspices, with a plaque urging respect for those buried there, but these accounts must be cited carefully as contemporary reportage rather than institutional history.[^51][^52][^53]

The exact number of people buried on Spinalonga during the leprosarium years remains uncertain; some articles estimate “over 400” or “around 1500” but without clear archival backing. Pending archaeological or municipal‑cemetery documentation, the article should avoid numerical claims and describe the cemetery instead as an integral part of the settlement’s physical and emotional landscape.[^54][^48]

## Relationship with Elounda and Plaka

Elounda and Plaka provided the primary mainland interface for Spinalonga in most periods, but the nature of that relationship changed significantly over time. In the Ottoman period, Spinalonga was itself a Muslim town with commercial ties across Mirabello; its boats and traders connected to markets and ports around the bay, and its status as a municipality underscored its centrality in local economic networks.[^44][^39][^4][^25]

With the onset of the leprosarium, the direction and meaning of connections shifted. The Cretan and then Greek State organised transfers of patients, staff and supplies by sea, primarily from ports near Heraklion and from Mirabello, but systematic records of boat licences, contracts with suppliers, and municipal arrangements in Elounda and Plaka have not yet been assembled. Journalism and oral histories point to complex emotional and economic ties: boatmen and their families relied on the institution for income; local traders sold goods; and at times residents of Plaka and Elounda expressed both compassion and fear towards the people across the water. Until specific oral‑history and municipal sources are brought into the project, these links must be described in general terms only.[^8][^45][^39][^18]

In the present tourism phase, boats from Elounda, Plaka and Agios Nikolaos carry large numbers of visitors daily to Spinalonga during the season, but the proposed article will deliberately avoid practical visit information and focus instead on how visibility across the gulf, proximity, and daily crossings have shaped perceptions of the island from surrounding communities over time.[^55][^21]

## Medical change and closure

The scientific turning point in Spinalonga’s medical history was the introduction of sulfone‑based therapy (notably dapsone and related drugs) in the 1940s, following international developments in Hansen’s disease treatment. The Archives of Hellenic Medicine article emphasises that the leprosarium operated primarily as a place of isolation rather than cure until these drugs became available; afterwards, treatment outcomes improved significantly and the rationale for permanent segregation weakened.[^36][^18]

Greek and international accounts agree that by the mid‑1950s many patients on Spinalonga were receiving effective treatment and that the institution closed in 1957, with remaining residents moved to Agia Varvara in Attica, though the exact sequence of transfers and the administrative closure order have yet to be traced in Ministry of Health or prefectural files. Popular narratives often speak of “the last 20 patients” or “the last inhabitant, a priest, leaving in 1962”, but these figures derive from journalistic or travel writing rather than from official records and should not be repeated as facts without corroboration.[^19][^56][^33][^53][^18]

The closure did not, however, end stigma or the social consequences of confinement. Many former residents continued to live with the physical and psychological effects of Hansen’s disease and of decades of exclusion, and some remained in institutions rather than returning to their original communities. Incorporating testimonies from these individuals, when permissions and ethical safeguards are in place, will be crucial for any future human‑centred narrative.[^57][^18]

## Abandonment, archaeology and heritage

After 1957, the buildings and walls of Spinalonga were left largely unattended; by the late 20th century, partial collapses, unauthorised interventions and demolition of some leprosarium‑era structures had altered the settlement fabric. A Greek cultural‑heritage platform records that much of the organised construction from the leprosarium phase was demolished in the 1980s, leaving only some key buildings—such as the hospital, certain houses and service structures—standing among the more robust Venetian and Ottoman remains.[^22][^5]

From the 1970s onwards, legal protection for both the natural landscape and built heritage expanded, and the Ephorate of Antiquities of Lasithi took on responsibility for conservation, excavation, interpretation and visitor management. Conservation projects have stabilised fortification walls, restored selected houses, created exhibition spaces (including an Ottoman‑era shop row and leprosarium‑related displays), and signposted key features, while ongoing debates around UNESCO nomination have spurred the production of management plans and further documentation.[^58][^59][^21]

In public memory, Spinalonga has often been framed through novels, television adaptations and “dark tourism” narratives that foreground suffering and isolation, frequently using formulas such as “island of the living dead” or “Europe’s last leper colony”; institutional and scholarly voices increasingly argue for a broader lens that gives equal weight to its Ottoman urban phase, Venetian military history and post‑closure heritage politics. The planned article will align with this more balanced approach, avoiding sensational language and grounding its account in verifiable sources.[^60][^51]

## Name and etymology assessment

### Spinalonga and Kalydon

Modern administrative usage treats “Kalydon” as the official Greek name for both the island and the associated peninsula, with “Spinalonga” strongly dominant in everyday speech and heritage discourse. Some encyclopaedic entries describe the area as the Kalydon archipelago, comprising the Spinalonga island, the Spinalonga peninsula (also called Kolokytha) and nearby islets, all formally under the name Kalydon. The Codex recommendation—to use **Spinalonga** in the article as the historical and local name, and to introduce **Kalydon** carefully with supporting maps and gazetteers—remains appropriate.[^61][^62]

### Etymological explanations

Several explanations circulate for the name “Spinalonga”:

- A Venetian‑era derivation from the phrase “stin Elounda” (“to Elounda”), allegedly misheard and reshaped by Venetians into *Spinalonga* or *Spina Longa*, sometimes linked to a similarly named island near Venice.[^26][^27]
- A direct Italian origin from *spina lunga* (“long thorn”), interpreted either as a figurative reference or a folk etymology attached later to explain the sound of the name.[^63][^64]
- A supposed reference to the island’s shape resembling a thorn or a spinal form.[^64]

Dawkins’ early 20th‑century Mirabello notes are reported in secondary literature as favouring a derivation from local Greek speech (*stin Elounda*) transmitted into Venetian documents, but access to the original text is still needed to confirm this reading. Tourism and popular‑history sites freely mix the *spina lunga* and *stin Elounda* stories, often stating both without weighing linguistic or documentary evidence.[^23][^65][^64]

On current evidence, the safest position is:

- Treat the *stin Elounda* → *Spinalonga* pathway as **plausible but unproven** without direct Venetian‑era documentation.
- Describe *spina lunga* and shape‑based explanations as **secondary or folk etymologies**, which may have influenced later interpretations or popular imagination but are not documented as original name‑giving motives.[^63][^64]

The article should therefore summarise these stories, attribute them clearly, and avoid presenting any as definitive.

## Unresolved questions and publication gate

Despite substantial progress, several high‑priority questions remain unresolved and currently block full drafting:

- **Original legal texts**: high‑quality facsimiles or critical editions of Law 375/1901, Law 463/1903, Decree 166/1903 and the internal‑operation decree must be obtained to confirm wording, signatories, and any provisions with interpretive weight.[^30][^13]
- **First transfer documentation**: a primary record (transport manifest, port log, or official report) is needed to fix the exact date and list of the first arrivals, beyond the widely cited but still secondary 13 October 1904 figure.[^16][^8]
- **Ottoman demographic and property record**: direct consultation of the 1881 census, municipal registers and Ottoman archival material is necessary to solidify population, family and occupational data for the 18th–19th centuries and to avoid over‑reliance on nomination texts and tourism summaries.[^4][^25]
- **Local economic and social links with Plaka and Elounda**: municipal archives, boat licences, local press and oral‑history projects from these communities need to be incorporated so that resident–mainland relationships are described from local perspectives rather than inferred.
- **Infrastructure chronology**: technical records or institutional accounts should be located to establish dates for installation of electricity, water systems, schools and other services on Spinalonga and, where relevant, their comparison with Plaka and Elounda.[^50][^74]
- **Expanded testimonies**: at least two additional named residents (beyond Remoundakis) and one mainland‑connected worker or caregiver should be documented through verifiable oral histories or archival material to support a plural, human‑scale narrative.
- **Cemetery and burial records**: archaeological reports, church and municipal records are required to clarify burial practices, approximate numbers and the sequence leading to the creation and rebuilding of the ossuary.[^52][^53]

Given these outstanding points, the recommendation remains that the project is **conditionally ready**: the structural and evidentiary foundations for the future article are strong enough to begin detailed planning, but the article should not be fully drafted or published until the core legal texts, key demographic records and additional testimonies have been secured and integrated.
