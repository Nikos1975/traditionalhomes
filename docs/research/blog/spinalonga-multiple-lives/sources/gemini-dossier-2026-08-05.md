# **Spinalonga Deep-Research Investigation: Architectural Stratigraphy, Spatial Segregation, and Institutional Memory**

## **Executive Assessment**

The islet of Spinalonga—officially recorded in modern Greek administrative registers as Kalydon—represents a physical palimpsest located at the northern entrance of Elounda Bay in eastern Crete1. Rather than an isolated monument to human suffering or an immutable Venetian fortress, the site is best understood as a continuously adapted architectural shell1. Applying the spatial-historical methodology of analyzing physical structures through successive functional transformations demonstrates how a single geographical place was repurposed across six primary historical epochs: a 7th–8th century Byzantine maritime watchpost, a late 16th-century Venetian fortress, a post-1669 Venetian sovereign enclave, a militarized Ottoman commercial town, a state-mandated Hansen’s disease leprosarium, and a contested modern public heritage site1.  
Spinalonga’s strategic significance derives entirely from its relationship to the surrounding marine topography1. Situated at the mouth of the natural harbor of Elounda and opposite the coastal settlement of Plaka, the islet commanded naval access into Mirabello Bay while providing visual surveillance over eastern Cretan shipping channels1. The physical structures surviving on the island today do not represent a single architectural campaign, but a continuous layering of stone masonry1. Venetian curtain walls and bastions were modified by Ottoman domestic additions, which were subsequently altered by the Cretan State and the Greek Ministry of Health to enforce compulsory medical segregation1.  
The existing baseline established in popular literature and initial project dossiers contains notable inaccuracies, romanticized distortions, and administrative misinterpretations1. Most prominently, claims that Spinalonga was artificially created by Venetian engineers cutting a sea channel, assertions of an ancient Hellenistic acropolis directly beneath the main fort, popular retellings claiming complete archival destruction upon closure, and widespread travel media asserting current UNESCO World Heritage inscription are factually incorrect1.  
The publication gate for the final public article on traditional-homes.gr remains **blocked**1. Public drafting cannot proceed responsibly until original Cretan State legislative facsimiles are secured, primary archival logs for final patient transfers are retrieved, visual asset permissions are cleared in writing, and numerical inconsistencies across official censuses are fully reconciled11.

## **Strategic Geography and Maritime Control**

Spinalonga’s historical evolution was dictated by maritime topography1. Positioned directly at the northern mouth of Elounda Bay, the islet commands a narrow sea passage separating the landward Kalydon peninsula from the main Cretan coastline at Plaka1. This placement allowed whoever controlled the island to regulate all naval traffic entering the sheltered, deep-water anchorage of Elounda Harbor while commanding visual surveillance over the western approaches of Mirabello Bay1.  
Geological and coastal surveys refute the persistent narrative that Venetian engineers physically severed a land isthmus to transform Spinalonga from a peninsula into an island1. Spinalonga has existed as a natural, detached limestone islet since antiquity1. Venetian engineering works further south at the Poros isthmus modified shallow passages for small craft, but the islet itself was formed by prehistoric sea-level changes and tectonic movements in the Aegean basin1.  
Archaeologically, assertions that the Venetian fortress directly overlay a Hellenistic acropolis belonging to the submerged city-state of Olous must be qualified3. Olous was centered at modern Poros Elounda3. While the islet of Spinalonga served as an outer lookout point during antiquity, the earliest surviving physical wall fragments beneath the Venetian ramparts date to the 7th and 8th centuries CE3. These early fortifications were constructed by the Byzantine Empire as an emergency defensive network against Arab naval raids3.  
The strategic geography of the island was also tied to regional resource extraction7. The shallow lagoon waters of inner Elounda Bay hosted salt pans28. During the Venetian period, salt was a strictly controlled state monopoly essential for food preservation and naval provisioning7. While defending the salt pans formed a component of the local economic rationale, Venetian Senate correspondence confirms that the decision to build a major fortress in 1579 was driven primarily by broader imperial strategy: securing deep-water fleet anchorages against the Ottoman navy following the loss of Cyprus in 15711.

## **Venetian Architectural and Construction Phases**

The construction of the Venetian fortress unfolded across distinct architectural campaigns, adapting military engineering to terrain limitations and evolving gunpowder artillery5. The initial campaign began in June 1579 under the direction of engineer Genese Bressani1. Bressani’s plan focused on securing the island’s perimeter against hostile landings by constructing a continuous lower ring wall following the irregular shoreline, punctuated by coastal half-bastions and demilunes5. Prominent elements of this first phase included the Moceniga Demi-lune commanding the southern channel and the Michiel Demi-lune positioned on the northern tip5.  
In 1584, Latino Orsini, Military Commander of Crete, conducted a thorough inspection of the ongoing works5. Orsini recognized a tactical flaw: Bressani’s lower enclosure was exposed to high-angle artillery fire from the elevated hills of the adjacent landward peninsula5. To counter this vulnerability, Orsini revised the structural plan between 1585 and 15865. He directed the construction of an upper fortification line along the rocky crest of the island, linked to the lower perimeter by transverse curtain walls5. This created a tiered defense system capable of firing over the lower batteries toward landward and seaward threats alike5.  
During the Cretan War (1645–1669), a third construction phase reinforced the site1. Engineers added thick earthen ramparts behind vulnerable masonry sections, constructed subterranean bombproof gunpowder magazines, enlarged rainwater cisterns, and reinforced artillery embrasures1. The completed fortress incorporated double rows of curtain walls, sally ports, and integrated gun platforms housing up to 35 cannons, forming an imposing defensive complex1.

## **The Post-1669 Venetian Enclave**

When the Ottoman Empire captured Candia (Heraklion) in 1669 after a 21-year siege, the negotiated Treaty of Candia contained an exceptional clause: Venice retained sovereign ownership over three isolated fortress islands—Spinalonga, Souda, and Gramvousa1. For 46 years (1669–1715), Spinalonga functioned as an isolated Venetian sovereign enclave surrounded entirely by Ottoman-controlled Crete1.  
During this enclave period, the island served three critical functions1:

* It provided a deep-water port of call for Venetian naval squadrons patrolling the Eastern Mediterranean and monitoring Ottoman maritime movements1.  
* It functioned as a sanctuary for thousands of Cretan Christians, insurgents, and political refugees fleeing Ottoman rule on the mainland1.  
* It supported a dense civilian settlement that expanded rapidly inside the walls, leading to the creation of seven distinct residential quarters served by seven Orthodox and Catholic churches1.

Managing supply networks during this phase was precarious1. Grain, arms, and timber had to be shipped directly from Venetian territories in the Ionian Islands and Venice itself, supplemented by clandestine trade with mainland Cretan vendors1. The enclave era ended during the Eighth Ottoman-Venetian War1. In the summer of 1715, a large Ottoman land force and naval fleet besieged Spinalonga1. After a three-month siege, with supply lines severed and water cisterns depleted, General Provisioner Zuan Francesco Giustiniani surrendered the fortress on 4 October 17151. Under the terms of capitulation, the Venetian garrison and civilian refugees were permitted passage to Venetian-held Corfu, though contemporary accounts record that many Christian refugees were subsequently captured or enslaved1.

## **The Ottoman Settlement: Demographics, Trade, and Civic Fabric**

Following the 1715 conquest, the Ottoman Empire converted Spinalonga into a military garrison and civilian settlement1. The strategic mandate shifted from defending against Ottoman expansion to securing Mirabello Bay against Christian corsairs and establishing imperial administrative control over eastern Crete1. An official customs office (*gümrük*) was established on the island in 17181. Over the 18th and 19th centuries, Spinalonga developed into a prominent Muslim commercial center in eastern Crete, offering security for Muslim families, merchants, and mariners during periods of intercommunal unrest on the Cretan mainland1.  
Architecturally, the Ottoman population transformed the Venetian military layout into a dense domestic town1. Over 200 two-story stepped stone houses were constructed along the sheltered western and southern slopes of the island, flanking narrow cobbled streets1. The ground floors of these structures typically housed commercial shops, olive oil storage pits, or craft workshops, while upper levels served as living quarters1. The main Venetian church was converted into a mosque with a minaret, and two educational institutions were established: an elementary primary school and a two-year *rüştıye* (Ottoman middle school)1.  
The economic profile of Ottoman Spinalonga was tied to maritime export trade1. Operating as an official port of entry, the town exported regional agricultural products, including olive oil (primarily for Marseille’s soap factories), leather, honey, beeswax, carobs, almonds, grindstones, and salt from the Elounda salt pans1. The 1881 Ottoman-Cretan census recorded 227 families totaling 1,112 Muslim residents on Spinalonga, confirming its status as the largest urban Muslim enclave in the Mirabello region1.  
The demise of the Ottoman settlement occurred rapidly between 1898 and 19041. Following the 1898 Heraklion massacres and the subsequent intervention of the Great Powers, French and British troops established protectorate sectors in eastern Crete37. As the autonomous Cretan State was formed under Prince George, the political environment became increasingly hostile to the remaining Muslim population9. Economic blockades by mainland Cretan insurgents, combined with state expropriation threats, forced Muslim families to emigrate to Heraklion, Izmir, Istanbul, and Rhodes9. By late 1903, only a handful of Muslim residents remained on the island, paving the way for the Cretan State to confiscate the domestic fabric for a new institutional purpose9.

## **Legislative Transition to Compulsory Medical Segregation**

The transformation of Spinalonga from an Ottoman town into a state-mandated Hansen’s disease leprosarium was engineered through specific legislative acts passed by the autonomous Cretan State9. On 30 May 1903, the Cretan Parliament enacted **Law 463** ("On the Settlement of Lepers in Crete"), which was officially published in the *Government Gazette of the Cretan State* on 7 June 1903 (Issue 25\)19. Law 463 established the legal framework for compulsory, lifelong segregation of all individuals diagnosed with Hansen’s disease within the borders of Crete and formally designated the islet of Spinalonga as the sole site of confinement9. This primary statute was supplemented by **Executive Decree 166** on 18 November 1903, which outlined internal administrative regulations, security guarding protocols, and property liquidation procedures9.  
Archival records reveal that the Cretan State Council selected Spinalonga based on four strategic calculations9:

* **Absolute Segregation**: The surrounding sea channel ensured isolation from the healthy population while rendering unauthorized escape difficult9.  
* **Efficient Guarding**: The existing Venetian perimeter walls and narrow maritime access allowed security guards to control the entry and exit of hundreds of individuals with minimal personnel9.  
* **Expropriation of Property**: Reusing the recently abandoned Muslim town eliminated the need to construct expensive new institutional barracks, allowing the state to expropriate over 200 homes at minimal cost1.  
* **Nationalist Alignment**: The designation served as a mechanism to force the final departure of the remaining Muslim residents, clearing the island for state control9.

The transfer process was executed coercively9. For centuries, Cretan Hansen’s disease sufferers had lived in marginal shanty settlements known as *meskinies* located outside the urban walls of Chania, Rethymno, and Heraklion10. On 13 October 1904, gendarmerie forces rounded up residents from these mainland camps and forcibly transported 251 patients (148 men and 103 women) by sea to Spinalonga23. Upon arrival, patients were allocated rooms inside the former Ottoman stone dwellings1. The state provided no initial medical treatment, viewing the colony purely as an isolation center rather than a therapeutic hospital10.

## **The Leprosarium: Early Institutional Neglect and Structural Isolation**

The first two decades of the leprosarium (1904–1925) were characterized by institutional neglect, severe physical deprivation, and high mortality rates10. The Cretan State—and after the 1913 Union, the central Greek government—provided patients with a meager daily cash stipend (initially 20 to 80 lepta) intended for purchasing basic foodstuffs22. However, supply chains were unreliable, and food quality was poor10. Spinalonga possessed no natural freshwater springs or deep wells35. The population depended entirely on rainwater collected in ancient Venetian cisterns, which were frequently contaminated, leading to outbreaks of waterborne diseases30. Water brought by boat from mainland Crete was rationed strictly and often spoiled during stormy weather30.  
The medical infrastructure was virtually non-existent during this early phase10. Doctors from mainland Neapoli or Agios Nikolaos visited the island only periodically upon request, refusing to reside on the islet out of fear of infection29. Patients were forced to nurse one another, perform basic wound dress-changes without painkillers, and bury their own dead10. Vital statistics illustrate the harshness of conditions: between October 1904 and December 1926, out of 730 total registered admissions, 465 patients died on the island, while 16 escaped and only 28 experienced spontaneous clinical remission25.  
Following Crete’s formal union with Greece in 1913, the facility was designated a National Leprosarium10. Patient intake expanded beyond Crete to encompass individuals seized by police authorities from across mainland Greece, the Aegean Islands, and overseas Greek diaspora communities10. This sudden population influx caused severe overcrowding in the remaining Ottoman housing stock10.  
Institutional turning points began to emerge in the late 1920s29:

* In **1925**, Dr. Emmanouil Grammatikakis was appointed resident medical director5. He advocated for systematic state funding, organized basic sanitary services, and dispatched regular analytical reports to the Ministry of Health detailing patient distress10.  
* In **1927**, Dr. Charles Nicolle, Director of the Institut Pasteur de Tunis and 1928 Nobel Laureate in Medicine, conducted an official inspection visit to Spinalonga5. Nicolle arranged for the donation of 10,000 doses of *Hygranol* (a purified chaulmoogra oil derivative), marking the institution’s first organized therapeutic intervention5.  
* Between **1928 and 1929**, the former Ottoman mosque was repaired and reconfigured to house a medical receiving ward, pharmacy, and administrative store29. Simultaneously, the Greek state established the Agia Varvara Anti-Leprosy Station in Athens to house less critical cases, establishing a twin-track institutional framework29.

## **Resident Life, Civic Organization, and Political Agency**

Despite compulsory isolation and severe physical distress, the residents of Spinalonga built a structured civic society, established self-governing institutions, and waged sustained political campaigns against the Greek state10. A defining catalyst for collective action occurred in 1936 with the arrival of **Epaminondas Remoundakis**, a 21-year-old law student from Heraklion diagnosed with Hansen’s disease11. Remoundakis organized the residents into a legal union: the **"Brotherhood of the Sick of Spinalonga, Saint Panteleimon"**11. Operating with an elected executive committee, the Brotherhood functioned as the island's *de facto* municipal government11.  
Under the Brotherhood's leadership, residents utilized political tactics to force institutional reforms11:

* **Petitions and Strikes**: They dispatched formal legal memoranda to the Ministry of Health, organized hunger strikes, and hoisted black flags over the Venetian ramparts to attract news coverage47.  
* **Infrastructure Improvements**: In 1939, the Brotherhood directed state funds and patient labor to blast away rocky coastal outcrops and construct a paved perimeter ring road around the island, vastly improving mobility for disabled residents1.  
* **Utilities**: They secured a heavy-duty electric generator, bringing electric lighting to patient homes and streets years before many mainland Cretan villages were electrified47.  
* **Sanitation**: They forced the state to install disinfection boilers, regularly clean Venetian water cisterns, and establish a dedicated sanitation corps paid from municipal allowances10.

Civil society flourished inside the walls10. Residents operated private grocery stores, coffeehouses, barbershops, and tailoring workshops using a sanitized cash currency system10. Cultural life included an outdoor cinema, a patient-run theater company that produced classical plays, loudspeaker music broadcasts along the main street, and a primary school taught by educated patients10.  
Religious life centered on the Church of St. Panteleimon32. From 1947 until after closure, **Papa Chrysanthos Katsoulogiannakes**, an Orthodox hieromonk from Toplou Monastery, served voluntarily as the colony’s resident priest27. Papa Chrysanthos lived alongside the sick, administered Holy Communion from a shared chalice to dispel contagion fears, conducted funeral rites, and provided spiritual support31.  
Family formation persisted despite legal bans10. While the 1920 Hansen’s Disease Law prohibited marriage among patients, residents cohabited, and official regulations were gradually relaxed to permit civil unions inside the colony10. Between 1904 and 1926, 39 children were born on Spinalonga25. Public health protocols mandated that uninfected infants be removed from their mothers at six months of age and transferred to the Agia Sofia Orphanage in Athens for preventative monitoring and adoption, creating profound personal loss for resident parents25.

## **Mainland Supply Networks: Plaka, Elounda, and Regional Interdependence**

Spinalonga was not isolated from its regional geographic context1. The operational survival of the leprosarium depended upon continuous logistical interaction with the mainland settlements of Plaka, Elounda, and the broader Mirabello district1. The small coastal fishing village of Plaka, situated directly opposite Spinalonga across a 600-meter sea channel, served as the primary mainland gateway1. All food, firewood, medical supplies, mail, building materials, and personnel passed through the Plaka shoreline1.  
Economic dependencies shaped local life30:

* **Maritime Transport Contracting**: Local boatmen from Plaka and Elounda were licensed by the state to operate daily ferry runs to Spinalonga47. Transporting goods, medical staff, guards, and visiting relatives provided a vital source of income for local families during decades of regional poverty47.  
* **Agricultural Trade**: Farmers and merchants from Elounda, Fourni, and Neapoli brought fresh vegetables, fruit, meat, and olive oil to the Plaka pier45. Disinfected trade procedures were established: cash payment coins from the island were dropped into jars of vinegar or chemical disinfectant before being handled by mainland vendors30.  
* **Employment**: Mainland residents secured institutional employment on the island as security guards, administrative clerks, laundry workers, and construction laborers for major masonry works30.

Social dynamics between the mainland and the island were complex30. While intense fear of contagion and severe social stigma surrounded the leprosarium, close personal bonds developed between island residents and mainland families30. Relatives of patients frequently moved to Plaka to remain near their isolated family members, communicating via hand signals and visual cues across the narrow sea channel47.

## **Pharmacological Revolution, Clinical Cure, and Closure**

The clinical and institutional demise of Spinalonga was driven by international advances in pharmacology1. For decades, medical care relied on palliative treatments, primarily chaulmoogra oil injections, which were painful and yielded limited therapeutic efficacy10. In 1941, American researchers demonstrated the efficacy of synthetic sulphone compounds (specifically *Promin* and subsequently *Dapsone*) in halting the replication of *Mycobacterium leprae*3.  
Sulphone therapy was introduced systematically on Spinalonga in **1948**3. The results were transformative: within months of initiating daily oral sulphone regimens, highly contagious bacteriological lesions healed, physical deterioration ceased, and non-contagious clinical cures were achieved3.  
The availability of effective cure altered the legal necessity of compulsory segregation19. In September 1955, the Greek Parliament enacted **Law 3369**, which modernized public health legislation regarding Hansen’s disease, repealed forced confinement regulations, and authorized the discharge of non-contagious, cured individuals30.  
In **July 1957**, the Greek Ministry of Social Welfare officially deactivated the Spinalonga Leprosarium1. The vast majority of cured residents returned to their home villages or settled in urban centers30. However, a core group of approximately 30 to 34 severely disabled or elderly patients requiring ongoing reconstructive surgery and nursing support were transferred via naval transport to the Agia Varvara Hansen’s Disease Center in Egaleo, Athens26.  
Popular claims regarding the "last resident" of Spinalonga require historical nuance11. While the last clinical patients were evacuated in July 1957, the island was not instantly deserted31. Papa Chrysanthos remained living on the islet until 1962 to perform liturgical services, maintain the St. Panteleimon church, and fulfill traditional Orthodox memorial rites over the patient cemetery for five years post-closure31.

## **Post-Institutional Decay, Selective Restoration, and Heritage Memory**

Following its 1957 deactivation, Spinalonga entered a period of abandonment and structural decay1. State authorities initially ignored the site, allowing atmospheric erosion, illegal looting of building timber, and architectural degradation1. During the 1970s and 1980s, official state policy prioritized the island's Venetian fortifications while attempting to erase its institutional medical legacy1. In the 1980s, under the direction of regional heritage authorities, workers demolished numerous 20th-century wooden leprosarium structures, patient workshops, and temporary residential additions to uncover the underlying 16th-century Venetian masonry1. This selective restoration strategy created a tension between preserving elite military fortification fabric and honoring 20th-century social history1.  
Protective legal status evolved incrementally1:

* **1970**: Declared a Protected Natural Landscape by ministerial decree1.  
* **1976**: Designated an Organized Archaeological Site under the jurisdiction of the Ephorate of Antiquities of Lasithi1.  
* **1998**: Architectural structures across all historical periods (Venetian, Ottoman, and Leprosarium) designated protected historical listed monuments1.

Conservation efforts led by the Ministry of Culture have since adopted an integrated approach1. Restoration campaigns funded by European Union regional development funds have stabilized the Venetian bastions, restored the main Ottoman commercial street, and conserved surviving medical ward interiors1.  
Regarding international recognition, Spinalonga was submitted by Greece to the UNESCO World Heritage Tentative List in 2014 (Ref. 5866\)2. In 2019, an official nomination dossier was submitted for inscription under UNESCO cultural criteria13. However, at the 44th Session of the World Heritage Committee in 2021 (Decision 44 COM 8B.19), following an ICOMOS technical evaluation, the nomination was deferred back to the State Party to refine site management plans, visitor capacity frameworks, and buffer zone protection controls4. As of 2026, Spinalonga remains on the **UNESCO Tentative List** and is **not** an inscribed World Heritage Site2.  
In public memory, popular literature—most notably Victoria Hislop’s 2005 novel *The Island* and its 2010 Greek television adaptation—massively increased international tourist visitation, turning Spinalonga into the second most visited archaeological site in Crete after Knossos3. However, this media coverage introduced romanticized tropes that often obscure the historical realities of state coercion, institutional neglect, and political struggle10.

## **Etymology and Naming Conventions**

The etymological origin of the name **Spinalonga** has been the subject of linguistic evolution and popular folk etymology1. The ancient regional name for the landward peninsula area was *Kalydon*3. In modern Greek administrative gazetteers, the official municipal designation of the island remains *Kalydon*, though this name is rarely used in daily speech1.  
The dominant scholarly linguistic consensus traces "Spinalonga" to a Venetian phonetic corruption of local Greek speech1. During the early Venetian occupation (13th–14th centuries), Venetian administrators transcribed the local Greek locator phrase **"στην Ελούντα"** (*stin Elounda*, meaning "at Elounda") into Italian script as **Stinalonde**, **Stinalonga**, and eventually **Spinalonga**1.  
A widespread folk etymology asserts that Venetian cartographers named the island directly from the Italian words **spina lunga** ("long thorn") due to its elongated physical shape1. While Venetian officialdom embraced this wordplay—which mirrored the historic name of the *Spinalonga* (Giudecca) island in the Venetian lagoon—the literal "long thorn" translation represents a secondary linguistic folk adaptation rather than its primary origin1.

## **Historical Contradiction Audit**

Four major historical contradictions identified across tourism copy and academic literature require resolution11:

> 1. **Venetian Canal Cutting vs. Natural Islet**: Popular travel websites frequently assert that Venice cut a physical canal through a peninsula to create Spinalonga1. Geological surveys and Ephorate publications establish that Spinalonga is a natural limestone islet1. Venetian canal works were confined to shallowing passages at the Poros isthmus further south1.  
> 2. **Hellenistic Acropolis vs. Byzantine Defense Wall**: Older guidebooks claim the Venetian fortress directly rests upon the ancient acropolis of Olous3. Ephorate archaeological analysis confirms that Olous was centered at Poros Elounda, while the earliest wall fragments on Spinalonga date to 7th–8th century CE Byzantine anti-Arab coastal defenses3.  
> 3. **UNESCO Inscription vs. Tentative List**: Commercial travel media routinely describe Spinalonga as an inscribed "UNESCO World Heritage Site"11. Official UNESCO records confirm the site was placed on the Tentative List in 2014 (Ref. 5866), and its formal nomination was deferred in 2021 (Decision 44 COM 8B.19)2. It is not inscribed4.  
> 4. **Complete File Burning Myth**: Popular stories claim all patient administrative files and personal records were systematically burned by health authorities upon closure in 19579. Official ministry reports confirm that while 1980s clearing work demolished dilapidated wooden leprosarium barracks, institutional medical records were transferred to Ministry of Health archives1.

## **Recommended Future Article Architecture**

The future public historical article for traditional-homes.gr should follow an architecture based on spatial reuse (the Papantou study method)4:

> 1. **The Islet at the Mouth of Elounda’s Harbor**: Introduce Spinalonga through geography—its placement commanding Elounda Bay and Mirabello approaches, contrasting natural topography with popular canal-cutting myths1.  
> 2. **Why Venice Fortified Spinalonga**: Frame the 1579 construction decision within late 16th-century Ottoman naval expansion, gunpowder warfare, harbor security, and regional salt production5.  
> 3. **How the Fortress Worked**: Architectural analysis of Bressani’s 1579 coastal perimeter vs. Orsini’s 1585 upper ridge defenses, artillery casemates, and water collection cisterns5.  
> 4. **A Sovereign Enclave After 1669**: Examine the 46-year period (1669–1715) when Spinalonga remained a Venetian enclave, refugee haven, and supply post post-Treaty of Candia, ending with the 1715 siege1.  
> 5. **Spinalonga as an Ottoman Town**: Reconstruct 18th and 19th-century domestic and commercial life—garrison town, customs office, export trade port, stepped stone houses, schools, and mosque1.  
> 6. **State Coercion and Medical Isolation**: The transition to compulsory Hansen’s disease isolation—autonomy politics, Law 463 (1903), property expropriation, and the 1904 forced transfers6.  
> 7. **Life Inside the Walls**: Architectural adaptation of Ottoman housing, early deprivation, patient agency, the Brotherhood of the Sick, Remoundakis’s civil rights struggles, and community culture6.  
> 8. **The Mainland Lifeline**: Logistics connecting Spinalonga to Plaka and Elounda—boatmen networks, disinfected trade, local employment, and social dynamics across the channel6.  
> 9. **Treatment, Cure, and Closure**: Introduction of sulphone antibiotics (1948), clinical cures, Law 3369 (1955), July 1957 closure, and final transfers to Agia Varvara3.  
> 10. **From Abandonment to Public Heritage**: Post-1957 decay, 1980s selective demolition, archaeological conservation, current UNESCO Tentative List status, and media memory distortions1.
