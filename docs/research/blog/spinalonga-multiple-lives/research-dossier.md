# Spinalonga: why it was fortified and how the island’s uses changed

## Executive assessment

Spinalonga (officially associated with Kalydon) is a pre-existing rocky islet at the mouth of Elounda’s natural harbour, not an island made by Venice. Its location gave visual and maritime control at the junction of the sheltered Gulf of Elounda, the approaches from Mirabello, and the shore opposite Plaka. The surviving place is best understood as a palimpsest: earlier walling, a late-sixteenth-century Venetian seaward fortress, a Venetian-held refuge after 1669, an Ottoman military-commercial settlement, a state isolation institution for people with Hansen’s disease, and an archaeological/heritage site.

The research record supports a future article organised around changes of use and physical adaptation rather than a generic chronology. It is not yet ready for drafting: the original legal texts, a fuller Ottoman social-history corpus and primary local testimony remain outstanding. Six user-owned modern photographs now have documented commercial permission—one proposed hero and five supporting images—with WebP derivatives prepared in research staging. Archival image, map and plan rights remain outstanding.

## Geography and strategy before the fortress

The island’s importance began with position, not masonry. It lies at the mouth of Elounda’s sheltered harbour, opposite the north-facing coast at Plaka and close to the Kalydon/Spinalonga peninsula. That placement lets a fortification watch movement between the enclosed water of Elounda and the wider Mirabello approaches. It also makes the island a continuing visual reference from Elounda, Plaka and the sea route.

Olous, near the Elounda/Poros side, supplies the regional ancient context. It does **not** justify an unqualified claim that the Venetian fortress replaced an ancient fortress. The Ephorate’s current interpretation places the earlier surviving Spinalonga wall in the 7th–8th centuries CE; a specialist archaeological publication should be obtained before using a more detailed pre-Venetian narrative. Claims that a channel was cut to “create” the island are not supported by the sources reviewed and must not be used.

## Construction and defensive system

The fortress followed terrain and gunpowder warfare. A lower perimeter followed the shore; an upper line occupied the ridge; transverse walls linked the two. Bastions, demilunes, cannon openings, gates, batteries, cisterns and garrison facilities formed a system rather than isolated monuments. Bressani’s lower scheme did not proceed unchanged. Orsini judged it vulnerable from the nearby peninsula and redirected work toward a second, higher defence zone. Extensive but less elaborate wartime alterations followed after the Ottoman invasion of Crete in 1645.

The future article may say that harbour protection, movement through the bay and the broader Ottoman threat formed a combined strategic setting. It should not reduce the decision to a single purpose such as salt-pans protection until archival evidence establishes that causal hierarchy.

## Uses, inhabitants and physical adaptation

### Venetian enclave after 1669

Spinalonga, Souda and Gramvousa remained Venetian under the 1669 terms, while the rest of Crete was Ottoman. The future piece needs evidence of provisioning, administration, civilian/refugee populations and relationships with Ottoman-controlled mainland Crete. The 1715 surrender ends this phase; its coercive aftermath needs cautious, properly attributed treatment.

### Ottoman settlement

The official record describes repair, settlement by Janissaries and families from 1718, a customs office from 1718 (with interruptions to 1866), trade in products including salt, leather, honey, wax, grindstones, silk and carobs, and a nineteenth-century commercial role. UNESCO’s nomination text reports 1,112 residents in 1881, about 200 houses and 25 shops/workshops at the end of the nineteenth century. These figures are usable only with attribution pending census/property-study verification. Stepped houses on the west and south sides, shops and harbour-facing activity are central physical evidence.

### Isolation institution, 1903–1957

The Cretan State selected an island with abandoned Ottoman housing for compulsory isolation—a decision linked in the official account both to prevailing medical policy and to removal of the remaining Muslim population. Residents were not merely passive recipients of confinement: they made homes, relationships, services and collective demands within severe constraints. The evidence reviewed supports sharply distinguishing the deprived early years from later improvements. It also supports linking the settlement to the mainland only where records do: transport, provisioning and medical/administrative connections must be investigated through local records and testimony rather than reconstructed as scenes.

Epaminondas Remoundakis is significant but cannot stand for all residents. An inscription records the 1939 perimeter-road opening through the Brotherhood of the Sick of Spinalonga and its president Remoundakis. Other assertions about a school, electricity, commerce, marriages, burial practices, final resident and everyday routines need person-specific or institutional evidence before publication.

### Heritage and public memory

After closure, Spinalonga was abandoned. The 2023 government cultural record says the surviving leprosarium buildings are the only remaining traces of that period’s organised construction because other buildings were demolished in the 1980s. Conservation and interpretation must therefore address absence as well as remains. The heritage narrative should give Ottoman settlement and residents with Hansen’s disease equal historical seriousness, rather than turning the site into one undifferentiated tragedy.

## Names and terminology

Use **Spinalonga** for the historic island in normal prose and explain **Kalydon** as the official/local administrative name only after primary cartographic or gazette confirmation. The “στην Ελούντα / stin Elounda” explanation is reported by historical accounts but requires Venetian-document citation; classify it as plausible, not established. The Italian word-play “spina lunga” and shape story are folk or secondary explanations unless a scholarly linguistic source proves a contemporary Venetian derivation.

Use “people with leprosy,” “people with Hansen’s disease,” “residents” and “patients” as context requires. Historical institutional labels can appear in quotations or explanatory notes.
