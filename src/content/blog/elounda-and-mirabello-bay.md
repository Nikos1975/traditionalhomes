## Elounda is more than one place

The name Elounda is useful precisely because it is not always used in one narrow sense. Someone arranging a meeting on the waterfront may mean the coastal settlement around Schisma. A municipal document may mean the Municipal Community of Elounda. In this article, “the Elounda area” is used in the broader geographic sense: the connected landscape of the hillside villages, the low water around Poros, the Kolokytha side, and the northern coast facing Spinalonga.

Those meanings overlap, but they are not interchangeable. Keeping them apart makes the area easier to read. It also avoids the common habit of treating every nearby village or shore as though it belonged to the same official unit simply because it shares a road, a view, or a practical connection with Elounda.

At the centre of the official settlement picture is Schisma, the coastal seat identified by the Municipality of Agios Nikolaos for the Municipal Community of Elounda. It is the built-up coastal place many visitors picture when they hear “Elounda”: the harbour-side streets, the bay, and the route north towards Poros. Yet that picture is only the beginning. Behind and beside it are the hillside settlements, while the water and low ground ahead lead into a more complicated coastal geography.

The most accurate way to use the name is therefore layered. Elounda can be an official community; it can be the coastal settlement around Schisma; and it can be a convenient geographical expression for a connected local landscape. The article uses each meaning deliberately, rather than asking one of them to do all the work.

## Elounda within Mirabello Bay

Mirabello Bay is the broader frame for understanding this coast. It is a large regional bay and a long-standing historical landscape in eastern Crete, not a single local district with one fixed official edge. Its useful extent changes with the question being asked. A marine study, an archaeological survey, a view from a hillside, and a municipal map can all use “Mirabello” at somewhat different scales.

For this article, Mirabello Bay means the wider coastal setting that places Agios Nikolaos, the Elounda coast, the northern approaches around Spinalonga, and the surrounding slopes within the same regional field of water and land. That is a working geographical definition, not a claim about an exact administrative or hydrographic boundary.

Within that broader frame, Elounda Bay is more specific. Peer-reviewed marine research describes it as shallow and semi-enclosed, relatively isolated from the outer coastal area of Mirabello Bay. That distinction is important. Elounda Bay is not simply another name for the whole of Mirabello Bay; it is a more sheltered part of the wider coastal system.

The word *lagoon* is also useful only when used carefully. Here it describes the shallow, sheltered water and low salt-pan landscape around Poros. It is not being used as an official alternative name for either Elounda Bay or Mirabello Bay. The three terms point to related but different scales: the outer regional bay, the more enclosed Elounda Bay, and the low local water-and-land threshold around Poros.

That distinction explains why the route from the coast to the peninsula can feel so compressed. A road passes from settlement to salt pans, canal, windmills, water and narrow land in a short distance. The scenery is not a collection of unrelated sights. It is the visible structure of the place.

## Schisma and the hillside settlements

Schisma is the coastal focus, but Elounda's settlement pattern does not stop at the water. The local urban-plan study lists Schisma, Epano Elounda and Mavrikiano within the Municipal Community of Elounda. They should not be collapsed into one undifferentiated village. Their positions make different parts of the landscape legible.

Schisma occupies the coastal side. Epano Elounda and Mavrikiano belong to the settlement pattern rising away from it. This is less a neat line from “old” to “new” than a relationship between shore, slope and routes across the lower hills. From the higher ground, the coast and bay are part of the view; from the waterfront, the villages behind it show how close the upland settlement pattern remains.

An early twentieth-century field account also records Schisma, Kato and Epano Elounda, Mavrikiano and Plaka as a spatial sequence along this side of the coast. It is useful as a dated observation of how the places related on the ground, not as proof that their present administrative status or built form has remained unchanged. That difference matters: historical geography can illuminate a pattern without freezing it in time.

Mavrikiano is especially useful for seeing the transition between coastal Elounda and the hillside. It belongs to the Municipal Community of Elounda, but its setting is not the same as Schisma's. The [Mavrikiano distances and area guide](/blog/mavrikiano-distances-and-guide/) deals with practical local orientation; [Walking Around Elounda](/blog/walking-around-elounda/) explains how the character of the ground changes between village lanes, lower slopes and more exposed terrain. This article's point is simpler: the villages behind the waterfront are part of the same local settlement system, even when they are not experienced from the same level or in the same way.

Kato Elounda appears in the historic spatial record, but the article does not use it to make a fresh administrative classification. Its value here is geographical. Together with Schisma, Epano Elounda and Mavrikiano, it helps show that “Elounda” has long referred to more than a single waterfront point.

## The lagoon, Poros and the peninsula

Poros is the hinge in the local landscape. Archaeological research around ancient Olous places material on both sides of it, at the point where the Kolokytha side meets Crete. This narrow threshold is one reason the area cannot be understood by looking only at the built-up shore. Water, low ground and the peninsula have shaped where movement, settlement and work could take place.

The salt pans make that relationship visible. The Ephorate of Antiquities places them on the approach from Schisma towards the Spinalonga and Kolokytha side. Near Poros, three windmills stand by the canal. Neither fact needs to become a long industrial history here. Together they show how the shallow edge of the bay was used and adapted, and why this stretch feels different from an ordinary coastal road.

The canal is part of the present geography, but this article does not assign it a precise construction date or a responsible authority. Those details require firmer direct evidence than the current record provides. What can be said is less dramatic and more useful: the cut, bridge, water and low salt-pan ground make Poros a passage between the more enclosed Elounda side and the peninsula landscape.

Kolokytha is therefore not merely a beachward extension of Elounda. It is a peninsula whose relationship to the bay, Poros and the archaeological setting of Olous gives it a distinct role. The detailed history of this low working landscape belongs in [Elounda Salt Pans and the Poros Windmills](/blog/elounda-salt-pans-and-poros-windmills/). For walking conditions and the changing surface of the peninsula, the more appropriate companion is [Walking Around Elounda](/blog/walking-around-elounda/).

## Plaka and the northern coast

Plaka is closely connected to the Elounda coast in geographical terms, but it should not be described as though it were administratively identical with Elounda. The local urban-plan study lists Plaka under the separate Local Community of Vrouchas, rather than under the Municipal Community of Elounda. That is the kind of distinction that everyday regional language can obscure.

Its position on the northern coast nevertheless makes Plaka part of the same practical coastal sequence. It faces Spinalonga and sits beyond the lower land and settlement pattern around Poros. In this sense it belongs to the wider Elounda-area conversation: not because an official boundary disappears, but because shoreline, road and view connect the places.

Spinalonga gives that northern orientation a clear landmark. UNESCO documentation describes it as a rocky islet at the mouth of Elounda's natural harbour. Spinalonga’s position off the northern coast makes it an important geographic reference point between Plaka, the Elounda area and the approaches from Mirabello Bay. The island's complicated history deserves its own treatment rather than a compressed retelling here. [Elounda, Spinalonga, and Wartime Memory](/blog/elounda-wartime-memory/) considers one later historical layer with the necessary care.

The distinction also helps with Vrouchas. Vrouchas is not simply a northern suburb of Elounda; it is the separate local-community frame in which Plaka is listed. [Welcome to Elounda Traditional Homes](/blog/welcome-to-elounda/) offers a first-party introduction to the Mavrikiano and Vrouchas setting, while this article keeps to the geographic relationship rather than accommodation or visitor planning.

## How history shaped the present geography

Ancient Olous is one of the reasons the coast around Elounda carries so much historical weight. The Ministry of Culture identifies ancient Olous with the area of present-day Elounda and records underwater research along Elounda Bay, the Kolokytha peninsula and both sides of Poros. Modern geophysical surveys at Olous have mapped submerged and buried walls, structural remains and road-like features around the Poros area, adding detail to the archaeological landscape visible beneath the water. The remains are not a detached ancient backdrop. They show that the shore, narrow passages and shallow water that organise the modern landscape were also central to an earlier settlement geography.

That does not justify a simplified story in which one event explains everything. The current research record does not support a single precisely dated catastrophe for Olous, and this article does not claim one. It is enough to recognise that changing shorelines, use of the low ground and the long relationship between settlement and water have left a complex inherited landscape.

Later uses added further layers. The salt pans and the windmills at Poros point to the working use of the sheltered edge. Spinalonga remains a major geographic reference point on the northern coast. The present-day settlement pattern still places the shore at Schisma in close relation to the hillside villages and to the road north. A broad tourism-era explanation would not account for all of these relationships. Geography, administrative organisation and historical use each describe a different part of the present.

For a fuller chronological account, read [A Short Chronological History of Elounda](/blog/elounda-history-through-its-shoreline/). The purpose here is not to repeat its sequence of periods, but to show why the present-day map still holds together: shore and slope, enclosed bay and outer bay, village and peninsula, local community and wider region.

## Using the name “Elounda” accurately

Elounda is best understood as a set of related meanings. Officially, it is the Municipal Community that includes Schisma, Epano Elounda and Mavrikiano. In everyday spatial terms, it is also the coastal settlement centred on Schisma and the connected landscape that extends through Poros towards the peninsula. Plaka belongs to that connected northern coastal geography, while remaining part of the separate Local Community of Vrouchas.

Mirabello Bay is the broader regional frame around that local picture. Elounda Bay is the more enclosed bay within it; the lagoon language belongs to the shallow Poros landscape and should not erase the distinction. Once those scales are kept clear, the area becomes less confusing. Elounda is not one point on a map, but neither is it an unlimited label for everything nearby. It is a coastal community, a settlement pattern and a geographical expression shaped by water, low ground, peninsula and hills.
